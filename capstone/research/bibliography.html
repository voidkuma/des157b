Mowreader, Ashley. “How Excessive Phone Use Can Hinder Student Success.” Inside Higher Ed | Higher Education News, Events and Jobs, 2025, www.insidehighered.com/news/student-success/health-wellness/2025/12/18/how-excessive-phone-use-can-hinder-student-success
This article focuses on the relationship between excessive phone use and academic performance among college students. Ashley Mowreader discusses how constant phone distractions can negatively affect focus, productivity, sleep schedules, and overall student success. This source is especially useful for my project because my target audience is college students, allowing me to directly connect the research to the experiences of my users. The article also provides a perspective that feels relatable and realistic rather than overly scientific or difficult to understand. Mowreader writes for Inside Higher Ed, a credible publication focused on education and student well-being, which strengthens the reliability of the information. From reading this article, I concluded that excessive screen time is not only a personal habit but also an issue that can impact motivation, academic performance, and mental focus in everyday student life.

Salamon, Maureen. “Doomscrolling Dangers.” Harvard Health, 1 Sept. 2024, www.health.harvard.edu/mind-and-mood/doomscrolling-dangers
This article explains the concept of “doomscrolling” and explores the psychological effects that can result from constantly consuming negative content online. The source discusses how excessive scrolling can increase stress, anxiety, and dependency on digital stimulation. This resource is highly relevant to my capstone project because it provides research and statistics about how screen time affects the brain and emotional health. I also found the explanation of dopamine and reward cycles particularly helpful because I want my project to encourage users to reflect on their own behaviors and habits. Since the article was published by Harvard Medical School, it is a highly credible and trustworthy source supported by medical and psychological research. After reviewing this source, I realized that doomscrolling is not just a bad habit but something that can shape mental health and emotional well-being over time.

Brain. “What Excessive Screen Time Does to the Adult Brain.” Lifestyle Medicine, 31 May 2024, lifestylemedicine.stanford.edu/what-excessive-screen-time-does-to-the-adult-brain/
This article examines the psychological and behavioral effects of excessive screen time on adults. One of the most interesting parts of the article was how it asked readers to imagine what they would do with an extra hour in their day, encouraging reflection on how much time is spent on phones and digital devices. The article argues that many people instinctively choose screen-related activities over healthier alternatives such as exercise, cooking, or social interaction. This source is useful for my project because it connects screen time to both mental and lifestyle habits, which aligns with the reflective experience I want users to have on my website. Since the article was written through Stanford University’s Lifestyle Medicine program, it has strong credibility and is supported by research in psychology and health sciences. My conclusion after reading this article is that excessive phone use can slowly replace healthier routines and habits, often without users even realizing it.
